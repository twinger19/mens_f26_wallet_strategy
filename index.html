The Shift

The wallet is not emptying. It is thinning.

The story everyone tells is that the phone killed the wallet. The Federal Reserve’s numbers do not support it. Cash has held at 14% of payments for two consecutive years, 76% of Americans still carried cash on them in 2025, and five of every six cash payments are under $25. Cash stopped falling.

Tap-to-pay is now roughly 70% of face-to-face Visa transactions — but the overwhelming majority of those taps are a plastic card, not a phone. Phones are still only 14% of in-person payments. What changed is where the card gets tapped, not whether it exists.

Ask people directly and the picture is the same. Only 18% of mobile wallet users expect to stop carrying a physical wallet within five years; 64% expect they still will. Among Gen Z it is 25% — higher, but still a minority.

We are not competing with the phone. We are competing with thickness. The man who wants five card slots instead of twelve is still our customer — if we build what he now wants.

The Real Clock

The threat isn’t Apple Pay. It’s the driver’s license.

A wallet exists as long as it holds something a phone cannot. Payment has largely moved. ID is the last thing holding the object together — so the only question that matters for long-range planning is how fast ID is moving. The answer is: far slower than the headlines.

Watch two triggers, not the state count. First, the first state to make a mobile ID legally sufficient at a traffic stop without the plastic card — Georgia’s law enforcement reader mandate lands July 2027, inside our planning horizon. Second, biometric e-gates at airports moving beyond today’s PreCheck pilots. Either one is a genuine step change. Another state adding Apple Wallet support is not.

Even the most bullish forecast — ABI Research, 143 million Americans holding a mobile ID by 2030 — describes people holding both. Their own framing: a companion to the classic licence, never the sole star. The wallet has this decade. What it does not have is twelve card slots.

Who We Are Actually Competing With

Four different fights, and only one of them is on our fixture.

Prices below are live MSRP, checked September 2026 at the retailer or brand site. The four tiers do not meet each other — which is exactly the problem, because three of them are reaching our customer somewhere we are not.

$46 – $185

The same wall

Other licensed brands

Fossil is the only true like-for-like — $46–55 core, same doors, a deeper named-family assortment. Its leather segment fell 38% in FY2025 to $69.3M, harder than its watches (−7%) or jewellery (−22%) — and it has now told investors it will “reinvigorate” the category. Also here: Michael Kors $79.50–129.50, Bosca $90–185, Johnston & Murphy $55–70, Original Penguin $48.

Fossil puts RFID in the product name, not on the hangtag. It reads from three feet away and survives into every search result.

$18 – $50

The retailer’s own label

Private label

Goodfellow $18–20 at Target. Sonoma $30 flat at Kohl’s — but in PU and split leather, so it is beating us on spec, not on cost. Then the one that matters: Roundtree & Yorke $50 in soft nappa leather, a Dillard’s exclusive.

The $30 undercut is survivable — the material gap is arguable in front of a buyer. A $50 nappa exclusive sitting inside our own mid band, under Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger at $54.50, is not.

$65 – $195

The slim specialists

DTC brands, now wholesale

Ridge $95–195, and no longer DTC in any meaningful sense — Nordstrom, Scheels, Buckle, Best Buy, 1,000+ doors. Bellroy runs nine SKUs at Nordstrom from $65 to $169. Ekster has one Nordstrom pop-up, not distribution. Secrid is Europe-led.

Bellroy’s $85–95 core lands exactly on our premium band, at Nordstrom, with a lifetime warranty printed on the box.

$11 – $150

The phone aisle

MagSafe — where we just landed

Apple FineWoven $59 sets the anchor. MOFT $30–60, Peak Design $49.95, Mous $49.99, Journey $70–130, Ridge MagSafe $75–150, Michael Kors $79.50. Opening price is $11–20; the volume zone is $30–60.

Every one of these is merchandised as a phone accessory, not a wallet. A $59 wallet next to a $39 charger reads normal. Next to a $30 bifold it reads absurd. The aisle sets the reference price.

Correct this one before it comes up in the room

Apple did not discontinue its MagSafe wallet. It killed FineWoven cases in 2024 and all leather accessories in 2023 — the wallet survived both, and was refreshed in September 2025 in five colours at $59 with Find My, RFID shielding and iPhone 12-through-17 compatibility. Apple also sells MOFT’s competing wallet on its own store at $49.95. It is a competitor and a channel at the same time.

The squeeze is from three sides at once. Private label takes the floor at $30 and matches us at $50. The slim specialists take the ceiling at $95–195 with lifetime warranties. And the fastest-growing part of the category is in an aisle where leather-goods brands have almost no shelf presence. The licensed mid-band at $42–54.50 is the pressure point — and the MagSafe shipper is the only thing in the pipeline that puts us where the growth is.

One spec note for the shipper. Find My tracking has become table stakes above roughly $50 — Apple, MOFT, Journey, Nomad, Ekster and Secrid all carry it. A MagSafe wallet over $50 without tracking now looks under-specified. Peak Design is the exception that proves it: at $49.95 it drops both tracking and RFID and sells on capacity and a lifetime warranty instead.

How The Business Is Built

Three ways we sell. Only one of them is on the brand tab.

The portfolio is wider than nine logos, and the two additions are the ones with room to grow. Being on both sides of private label is an advantage if we plan it that way — and an awkward conversation if we do not.

01

Licensed

Nine brands, $42–95, the core of the business and the whole of the Brand Architecture tab. Royalty and licensor approval sit on every decision, and the mid-band is where the squeeze is hardest.

02

Private label

On the same fixture, private label competes directly with our licensed brands — and we manufacture some of it. That is a real hedge: when a buyer asks what a logo adds at $48 against their own $50 nappa exclusive, we would rather be on both sides of the answer than lose the linear foot entirely.

03

Non-branded

The newest and least defined. The MagSafe shipper is the first real test — three wallets, its own fixture, and packaging carrying materially more feature callouts than our standard wallet boxes. No licensor approval, no royalty, and it lands in the aisle where the category is actually growing.

What the MagSafe shipper already proved

We put more information on that packaging than we put on any licensed wallet box — because in the phone aisle, that is the standard. MOFT sells on magnet strength in newtons and tracking range in metres. Apple leads with Find My and RFID. Nobody in the wallet aisle quantifies anything. The shipper was not a one-off; it is the packaging standard the rest of the line should be held to.

Build to the price band

Kohl’s merchandises its gift shop by price — under $10, $15, $25 and $50 — not by product. A wallet priced at $52 is invisible in a $50 gift band. Every gift SKU should be built to land inside a band rather than just above one.

The boxed set is the format

In prestige fragrance, gift sets are more than 25% of all Q4 category sales. Different category, same mechanic: when a format is purpose-built for gifting, it concentrates the quarter. A wallet plus a card case or key fob at $45–55 reads as more considered than a gift card at the same ticket.

The box is the first thing a gift-giver buys. Make it worth buying.

Packaging & Display Strategy

We’ve built a better product. Now we need to tell the story.

The five categories above represent real investment in product design. At retail and online the customer never sees that thinking — because our packaging says nothing and our display does nothing. In a category where most of the decision is made standing at the shelf, that is not a design problem. It is a revenue problem.

Current State — The Problem

Plain box. No story. No features. No reason to buy over the one next to it.

  • ✕All product in closed boxes — customer must open every item to evaluate it
  • ✕No RFID callout, no capacity info, no slim profile claim, no material story
  • ✕Products organized by brand — not by how the customer shops (by need)
  • ✕No carry-type navigation — "Slim Carry", "Gift Ready", "Front Pocket" labels don't exist
  • ✕Empty shelf space signals clearance, not a living category
  • ✕Generic "Wallets" header — invisible next to other brands' destination displays three feet away
Where We Are → Where We're Going

Packaging and display that sell before the wallet is touched — in-store and online.

Packaging & Online

Good packaging communicates before the customer touches the product.

The goal isn't a beautiful box — it's a box that sells. That means three things on the front panel: what it holds (card capacity), what it does (RFID blocking), and what kind of carry it's built for. That same information should be consistent across every brand and every fixture, so the display and the package speak the same language.

Brand Identity SignalEvery package should communicate the brand's positioning — not just carry a logo. Material, texture, and typography do that work.

Feature HierarchyLead with what matters: RFID protection, card capacity, slim profile. These are the features that move product on a shelf without a sales associate.

Gift-Ready ConstructionClothing and accessories is the No. 1 gift category at both peaks. Packaging that looks gift-ready on the shelf removes the need for wrapping — and supports a higher price point.

And the PDP has to say the same thing.

A shopper who sees the wallet on the fixture and then looks it up online should feel one brand speaking. Lead the PDP with the product in hand rather than sitting in a box, then the same feature icons the package carries — RFID, card capacity, profile, material. The five carry-type names on the shelf rail should be the five filters in the online navigation.

The display teaches the customer the language. The PDP closes the sale. They have to use the same words.

The 2027 Plan

Four moves. Everything else is detail.

The evidence points in one direction: a smaller wallet, sold as a gift, decided at the shelf, with ID at the centre of the product story.

01

Build for five card slots, not twelve

Extra capacity keeps a real customer and stays in the line, but the growth is in Front Pocket and New Classic. Shift the SKU mix toward the thinner half of the carry ladder and stop treating slim as a niche.

02

Put ID at the centre of the product

The licence is the last thing the phone cannot take, and no state lets it go for at least this decade. Clear ID windows, quick-access ID slots and a real ID story on the package are the category’s most defensible feature.

03

Plan two peaks, and ship to the barbell

A dedicated Father’s Day programme — the fastest-growing occasion, and the one where department stores match online. Then a Q4 floor set in early October and a hard replenishment cut by 15 December.

04

Make the package do the selling

Card capacity, RFID and carry type on the front panel of every box. Carry-type rail labels on the fixture. One open wallet per tier. The same five words in the aisle and on the PDP.

What would change this plan: a state making a mobile ID legally sufficient without the plastic card, or biometric gates going mainstream at TSA. Both are worth a standing watch. Neither has happened.

These nine brands do not all sit in the same store, and they do not all compete with each other. Completeness Check

What this covers — and what is still missing.

Green is on every brand card above. Gold is the gap. None of the gold can be invented — they are inputs the team has to bring, which makes them the right pre-work to assign at the end of the meeting.

Covered

Price tier & key item

Live MSRP checked at Kohl’s, Belk and the brand sites in September 2026, marked verified or unverified per brand.

Covered

Materials & construction

Leather type, lining, hardware finish, edge treatment. This is what actually stops two brands from looking alike at three feet.

Covered

Consumer & gifting occasion

Who he is, and whether the brand is a self-purchase or a gift. Drives packaging spend and the Q4 plan.

Covered

Color story & guardrails

A five-color seasonal palette plus an explicit list of what the brand must not do — the half people skip, and the half that prevents overlap.

Covered

Channel model

Whether we sell brand-direct as well as wholesale, or wholesale only. Changes who approves the product and who we are really selling to.

Still needed

Three MSRPs still unconfirmed

Tommy Hilfiger, Calvin Klein and Haggar could not be verified — Macy’s blocks price scraping, and haggar.com does not separate list from promo. Pull these three off the line list before the meeting.

Still needed

SKU count and door count per brand

How many styles each brand earns, and at how many doors. Dockers already carries eleven silhouettes at one price; without a ceiling every brand asks for twelve.

Still needed

Licensor approval rules and royalty rate

Eight of these nine are licensed. What each licensor will and will not approve — logo use, materials, price floors, channel restrictions — and what the royalty does to margin. This kills more F27 ideas late than anything else here.

Still needed

F25 / F26 actual sell-through by brand

Units, AUR, sell-through and returns per brand, ideally per door. Merchandising should bring this.

Still needed

Competitive set on the wall

For each brand, who it loses to at its own doors — the other licensed brands, the retailer’s private label, and the direct-to-consumer slim-wallet players.

Still needed

Packaging and display expression per brand

Which brands get a rigid box, which get a hangtag, which get a fixture of their own.

Still needed

Replenishment versus seasonal split

Which SKUs per brand are year-round core and which are seasonal or gift-only. Changes the design brief and the inventory conversation.

Still needed

Sustainability and materials compliance

Recycled content, chrome-free tanning, state-level chemical regulation. Several licensors now require a materials disclosure.

Using This In The Room

Send the cards before the meeting. Spend the hour on four decisions.

Reading nine brand cards aloud will burn the room. The cards are reference. These four are the conversation.

01

Break the $42–54.50 cluster

Six of the nine brands live inside twelve dollars of each other, and all but Tommy Hilfiger carry a single price across every silhouette. Decide which brands tier up for F27 and which hold. This is the highest-value decision on the list.

02

Plant the MagSafe flag

Four brands are credible in phone companion — Calvin Klein, Kenneth Cole, Columbia and Tommy Hilfiger. Pick one to lead and one to follow, and make the rest white space. Spreading it kills the category in test.

03

Settle Dockers

The only brand here where the question is not design but whether we have a license under Authentic. Assign that answer to one person with a date before the meeting ends.

04

Assign the gaps

Three unconfirmed MSRPs and sell-through data to merchandising. Licensor rules and royalty to sales and legal. Competitive audit to whoever walks the doors. The architecture is not finished until those come back.
